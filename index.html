Multiple linear regression

The toddler height example, the Advertising dataset (200 markets) and first-order models with quantitative predictors.

1.1Simple regression: three versions of "the model"

We have pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) and want a straight line describing how \(Y\) changes with \(X\). \(X\) is the predictor (independent, explanatory variable). \(Y\) is the response (dependent variable).

ExpressionWhat it is
\(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\)Population model. Describes each individual observation, scatter included. The β's are true but unknown.
\(E(Y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\)Regression equation. The average Y at a given X. ε disappears because \(E(\epsilon) = 0\).
\(\hat{Y} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X\)Estimated model. The \(\hat\beta\)'s are calculated from our sample by least squares.

\(\beta_0\) is the intercept (Y when X = 0), \(\beta_1\) is the slope (change in Y per 1-unit increase in X), and \(\epsilon\) is everything affecting Y that isn't X.

Toddler example (12 toddlers, age 18–29 months)

\[\widehat{Height} = 60.25 + 0.848 \times Age\]

For each extra month of age, predicted (average) height increases by about 0.85 cm. The intercept, 60.25 cm at age 0, has no practical meaning: no toddler in the data is close to 0 months.

Remember: β is the true unknown value; β̂ is our estimate from the sample. We need the population model to say what we're trying to learn, and the estimated model is what we can actually compute.
Check yourself
  1. Interpret the 0.85 in plain English.For each extra month of age, predicted (average) height goes up by about 0.85 cm. Say "on average": the line describes the average toddler, not any one child.
  2. Why shouldn't this model predict a 6-month-old's height?The data only cover 18–29 months, so 6 months is extrapolation. Babies grow much faster in their first year, so the same line probably doesn't hold there.

1.2Sums of squares, R² and MSE

Take any data point and picture three vertical distances:

NameFormulaMeasures
SST (total)\(\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2\)How far the points are from the mean: total variation in Y
SSR (regression)\(\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2\)How far the line is from the mean: variation the model explains
SSE (error)\(\sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\)How far the points are from the line: variation left unexplained
\[SST = SSR + SSE\]

BI analogy: if your only "model" were AVG(Height) (80.175 cm here), SST measures how wrong that flat prediction is. The regression line removes part of the error (SSR), and what's left is SSE.

R², the coefficient of determination

\[R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}\]

R² is the proportion of the variation in Y explained by the model. For the toddlers: SST = 118.2, SSE = 15.4, SSR = 102.8, so R² = 0.87. "87% of the variation in height is explained by age."

Say this"75% of the variation in height is explained by the model."
Not this"75% of the model is explained by the variation in age."

MSE, the estimate of the error variance

\[MSE = \frac{SSE}{n-2}\]

We divide by \(n-2\) because two parameters (\(\beta_0, \beta_1\)) were estimated, and each uses up one degree of freedom. That makes MSE an unbiased estimate of \(\text{Var}(\epsilon)\). With p predictors the rule becomes \(n - p - 1\).

Remember: SST = SSR + SSE, and R² = SSR / SST is the share of the variation in Y that the model explains.
Check yourself
  1. SST = 200 and SSE = 50. Find SSR and R², and interpret R².SSR = 150 and R² = 0.75. 75% of the variation in Y is explained by the model; the other 25% is unexplained.
  2. A model has 3 predictors and n = 50. What do you divide SSE by to get MSE?\(n - p - 1 = 50 - 3 - 1 = 46\).
  3. Is R² the correlation coefficient?No. The correlation is r (between −1 and 1). R² is the coefficient of determination. In simple regression only, R² = r².

Each model tests \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (no linear relationship) against \(H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0\). Worked for newspaper:

\[F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} = \frac{282.34/1}{5134.805/198} = 10.89\]

The df are (1, 198): 1 predictor on top, \(n - 2 = 198\) underneath. On their own, all three media look significant.

Interpreting with units

TV slope 0.0475: every extra $1,000 on TV is associated with about 47.5 more units sold, on average. This is observational data, so say "associated with", not "causes".

The problem

Markets that spend more on radio also tend to spend more on newspaper; the predictors are correlated. Radio really drives sales, so the newspaper-only model gives newspaper credit for radio's effect. Newspaper acts as a stand-in for radio.

Remember: a one-predictor model can't separate overlapping effects. Multiple regression can, because it estimates each effect while holding the others constant.
Check yourself
  1. Why does each simple F-test have df (1, 198)?1 = the number of predictors. 198 = 200 observations minus the 2 estimated parameters (β₀ and β₁).
  2. Radio's 95% CI is (0.1622, 0.2427). How can you tell it's significant at α = 0.05 without the p-value?0 is not in the interval, so we reject H₀: β₁ = 0 at α = 0.05. A 95% CI and a two-sided test at 0.05 always agree.
  3. Newspaper is significant alone but not alongside TV and radio. Why?Newspaper is correlated with radio. Alone, it borrows radio's effect; once radio is held constant, it has nothing left to explain. This idea returns later as multicollinearity.

1.4The multiple regression model

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p + \epsilon = \mu + \epsilon\]

\(p\) is the number of predictors and \(\mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \dots + \beta_pX_p\) is the deterministic part, the predictable average. For Advertising:

\[Sales = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{TV} + \beta_2 X_{radio} + \beta_3 X_{newspaper} + \epsilon\]

A first-order model has each quantitative predictor on its own, to the power 1: no \(X^2\) terms and no \(X_1X_2\) products.

Why it's harder than simple regression

  1. Model selection: many possible subsets of predictors, several of which can look reasonable.
  2. Visualization: you can't plot beyond 3D.
  3. Overfitting: more predictors isn't always better; complex models fit noise.
  4. Computation: needs matrix algebra, which R does for you.

Geometry: 1 predictor gives a line, 2 give a plane through a 3D cloud, 3+ give a hyperplane. Least squares always minimizes the squared vertical distances to the surface.

Remember: "first-order" is about powers and products, not the number of predictors.
Check yourself
  1. Is \(Sales = \beta_0 + \beta_1 TV + \beta_2 TV^2\) first-order?No, because of the TV² term, which makes it second-order. Simple regression has one predictor and is first-order.

1.5Least squares and the design matrix

Choose the \(\hat\beta\)'s that minimize SSE:

\[SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \hat\beta_p x_{pi})\big)^2\]

The solution in matrix form:

\[\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\] \[X = \begin{pmatrix}1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np}\end{pmatrix}\]
ObjectAdvertising sizeWhy
\(y\)200 × 1One response per market
\(X\)200 × 4One row per market; columns 1, TV, radio, newspaper
\(X'X\)4 × 4The (p+1) × (p+1) matrix that gets inverted
\(\hat\beta\)4 × 1One estimate per parameter

The column of 1s produces the intercept, because \(\beta_0\) is multiplied by 1 on every row. Without it, the plane is forced through the origin, which distorts the slopes too.

X = model.matrix(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising)
y = Advertising$sale
solve(t(X) %*% X) %*% t(X) %*% y   # same as coef(lm(...))

TV barely moves because it is nearly uncorrelated with the other media. Newspaper collapses because it is correlated with radio.

Remember: X has n rows (observations) and p + 1 columns (parameters).
Check yourself
  1. What are the dimensions of X for Advertising?200 × 4. 200 markets; columns for the intercept, TV, radio, newspaper. (X′X is 4 × 4.)
  2. What goes wrong without the column of 1s?There's no intercept, so the fitted plane is forced through the origin (predicted Y = 0 when all X = 0), which also distorts the slopes.

1.7Confidence intervals for coefficients

\[\hat\beta_i \pm t_{\alpha/2,\; n-p-1} \times S_{\hat\beta_i}\]

\(S_{\hat\beta_i}\) is the standard error (the Std. Error column of summary()). We use a t-distribution because the error variance is estimated, not known. \(t_{\alpha/2}\) is the upper critical value (positive).

By hand: TV at 95%

df = 200 − 3 − 1 = 196, so \(t_{0.025,196} \approx 1.972\). Then \(0.045765 \pm 1.972 \times 0.001395 = 0.045765 \pm 0.00275\), giving (0.0430, 0.0485).

"We are 95% confident that each extra $1,000 on TV is associated with an increase of 43 to 48.5 units sold, holding radio and newspaper constant."

confint(predict_ad_model)                # 95%
confint(predict_ad_model, level = 0.99)  # 99%
Levelt (df 196)TV interval
95%1.97(0.0430, 0.0485)
99%2.60(0.0421, 0.0494)

"95% confident" means: if we repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true β. It doesn't mean a 95% chance that β is in this particular interval.

Newspaper's CI is (−0.0126, 0.0105). It contains 0, so its effect isn't distinguishable from zero. Before dropping it, confirm with a t-test or partial F-test, compare adjusted R² and RSE, and ask whether there's a business reason to keep it.

Remember: 0 inside the CI ⇔ fail to reject H₀: βᵢ = 0. Higher confidence means a bigger t, so a wider interval.
Check yourself
  1. Is the 99% CI wider or narrower than the 95% CI? Why?Wider. The t critical value grows from about 1.97 to 2.60, and multiplies the same standard error. More confidence costs precision.

1.9The global F-test and the ANOVA table

The first question: is the model useful at all?

\[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_p = 0 \qquad H_a: \text{at least one } \beta_i \neq 0\]

H₀ says the model is no better than the null model, lm(sale ~ 1), which predicts ȳ every time. For the null model SSR = 0 and SSE = SST.

Why test the whole model first?

Many separate t-tests inflate false positives: about 5 "significant" results per 100 useless predictors at α = 0.05. The global F-test is one test, so the overall Type I error rate stays at α.

SourceSSdfMean squareF
RegressionSSR\(p\)MSR = SSR / pMSR / MSE
ErrorSSE\(n-p-1\)MSE = SSE / (n−p−1)
TotalSST\(n-1\)
\[F_{obs} = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-p-1)} \sim F_{p,\;n-p-1}\]

If the predictors are useless, MSR ≈ MSE and F ≈ 1. A large F means the model explains far more than chance.

Advertising, from aov()

            Sum Sq   Df
tv        3314.618    1
radio     1545.617    1
newspaper    0.089    1
Residuals  556.824  196
  • SSR = 3314.618 + 1545.617 + 0.089 = 4860.324; MSR = 4860.324 / 3 = 1620.11
  • MSE = 556.824 / 196 = 2.841
  • F = 570.3, df (3, 196), p ≈ 0, so we reject H₀
  • SST = 5417.149 and R² = 4860.324 / 5417.149 = 0.897

If the global test fails, stop. Don't hunt through t-tests for something significant. If it succeeds, at least one predictor matters, but it doesn't say which.

Remember: both the SS column and the df column add up: SST = SSR + SSE and (n−1) = p + (n−p−1). Write the df first, then divide.
Check yourself
  1. n = 30, p = 4, SSR = 600, SSE = 250. Fill in the ANOVA table.df: 4, 25, 29. MSR = 150, MSE = 250/25 = 10, F = 15, SST = 850, R² = 0.71.
  2. Sanity check: could R² = 0.71 go with F ≈ 1.2?No. R² = 0.71 says the model explains a lot; F ≈ 1 says it barely beats the mean. When numbers contradict each other, recheck the arithmetic.
  3. The global F-test gives p = 0.40. What next?Fail to reject H₀ and stop. Searching the t-tests would invite false positives. Rethink the predictors or the model.

1.10Sequential SS in aov()

aov() assigns sums of squares in the order the predictors are listed. The first predictor gets credit for everything it explains alone; each later one gets only the extra it adds.

Newspaper last
sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper
Newspaper SS = 0.089
Newspaper first
sale ~ newspaper + tv + radio
Newspaper SS = 282.34 (its simple-regression SSR)

The individual SS change with order, but the total SSR (4860.324) doesn't.

Remember: aov() SS depend on order; t-tests in summary() don't.

1.11Individual t-tests

\[H_0: \beta_i = 0 \;\text{(given the other predictors)} \qquad H_a: \beta_i \neq 0\] \[T_{obs} = \frac{\hat\beta_i - 0}{S_{\hat\beta_i}} \sim t_{n-p-1}\]

The test statistic counts how many standard errors the estimate is from 0. The p-value is two-sided, \(2 \times P(T \geq |t_{obs}|)\); R prints it as Pr(>|t|).

1.12The partial F-test

Does a subset of q predictors add anything, given the others are already in the model?

  • Full model: all predictors
  • Reduced model: the full model minus the q predictors being tested
\[F_{obs} = \frac{(SSE_{reduced} - SSE_{full})/(df_{reduced} - df_{full})}{SSE_{full}/df_{full}} \sim F_{q,\;df_{full}}\]

df here means error df. Dropping predictors always increases SSE; the test asks whether it increases more than chance would explain.

reduced = lm(sale ~ tv + radio, data = Advertising)
full    = lm(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising)
anova(reduced, full)   # reduced model first
\[F = \frac{(556.9140 - 556.8253)/(197-196)}{556.8253/196} = \frac{0.0887}{2.841} = 0.031,\quad df = (1, 196),\; p = 0.86\]

Fail to reject, so drop newspaper. The final model is:

\[\widehat{Sale} = 2.921 + 0.04575X_{TV} + 0.18799X_{radio}\]

Three connections

  1. The SSE increase (0.0887) is newspaper's sequential SS (0.089), because newspaper was entered last.
  2. For one predictor, F = t²: (−0.177)² ≈ 0.031, with the same p-value.
  3. The partial F-test earns its keep when q > 1, e.g. radio and newspaper together beyond TV.
# Do radio and newspaper together add anything beyond TV? (q = 2)
anova(lm(sale ~ tv, data = Advertising),
      lm(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising))
Remember: the predictors you're testing are the ones missing from the reduced model. Report the df as a pair: (q, df_full).
Check yourself
  1. n = 40. Full model: 5 predictors, SSE = 120. Reduced: 3 predictors, SSE = 180. Test it.df_full = 34, df_reduced = 36, q = 2. F = (60/2) / (120/34) = 30 / 3.53 = 8.5 with df (2, 34). The 5% cutoff is about 4.1, so reject H₀: the dropped predictors matter. (Not "because SSE went up"—it always does.)
  2. To test radio and newspaper together beyond TV, what are the models and q?Full: TV + radio + newspaper. Reduced: TV only. q = 2.

1.13R², adjusted R² and RSE

R²

\[R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} = \text{Cor}(Y, \hat Y)^2\]

R² can never decrease when a predictor is added, even a useless one. It has no penalty for complexity, so it always favours the bigger model.

Adjusted R²

\[R^2_{adj} = 1 - \frac{SSE/(n-p-1)}{SST/(n-1)} = 1 - \frac{MSE}{SST/(n-1)}\]

Penalizes each extra predictor through its degrees of freedom, so it can go down. It's always ≤ R².

RSE, the residual standard error

\[s = RSE = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-p-1}} = \sqrt{MSE}\]

The typical size of a prediction error, in the same units as Y. Lower is better.

summary(full)$r.squared;     summary(reduced)$r.squared
summary(full)$adj.r.squared; summary(reduced)$adj.r.squared
sigma(full); sigma(reduced)
ModelSSEdfMSER²R²adjRSE
TV + radio + newspaper556.8251962.8410.897210.89561.686

Plain R² favours the full model by 0.00002. Adjusted R² and RSE both favour TV + radio. "Using TV and radio, our sales predictions are typically off by about 1,700 units."

Two useful facts

  1. Adjusted R² and RSE always agree on the same dataset: both are built from MSE.
  2. Dropping a predictor raises adjusted R² exactly when its |t| < 1. Plain R² goes down (or stays the same) whenever you drop one.
Remember: compare models with adjusted R² and RSE, never plain R². Report plain R² when you say "X% of the variation is explained".
Check yourself
  1. n = 30, p = 4, SSE = 250, SST = 850. Find R²adj and RSE.MSE = 250/25 = 10. SST/(n−1) = 850/29 = 29.31. R²adj = 1 − 10/29.31 = 0.659. RSE = √10 = 3.16. Adjusted R² (0.659) is below R² (0.71), as it must be.
  2. A predictor has t = 0.8. Drop it: what happens to R² and adjusted R²?Adjusted R² goes up (|t| < 1). Plain R² goes down or stays the same.

1.15Extrapolation

Only predict inside the range of the data used to fit the model. In Advertising, TV runs from about 0.7 to 296 and radio from 0 to about 50.

  • TV = 400 is outside the data, so the answer is no: we have no evidence the straight line holds there, and ad spending often has diminishing returns.
  • Hidden extrapolation: each value can be in range alone while the combination never appears (very high TV and very high radio together). Check a scatterplot of the predictors against each other.
summary(Advertising)                       # min / max of each column
plot(Advertising$tv, Advertising$radio)    # which combinations exist?
Remember: apply the range rule to the actual numbers and commit to an answer.

Interaction models

When the effect of one predictor depends on another: grandfather clocks, factories, Advertising with TV × radio, and two practice problems.

2.1Additive vs interaction models

An additive model gives each predictor one fixed effect. A one-unit increase in \(X_1\) changes Y by \(\beta_1\), whatever the value of \(X_2\). The effects simply add up:

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \epsilon\]

Interaction occurs when the effect of one predictor is not constant across the values of another. To model it, add a cross-product term:

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \beta_3X_1X_2 + \epsilon\]

Auction price ($) from the clock's age (years) and the number of bids:

\[\widehat{Price} = 320 + 1\,X_{Age} - 93\,X_{Bids} + 1.5\,X_{Age}X_{Bids}\]

Fix the number of bids and look at the slope of age:

2.2Testing for an interaction

Add the product term, then run a t-test on its coefficient:

\[H_0: \beta_3 = 0 \;\text{(no interaction)} \qquad H_a: \beta_3 \neq 0 \;\text{(interaction present)}\] \[T_{obs} = \frac{\hat\beta_3 - 0}{S_{\hat\beta_3}} \sim t_{n-p-1}\]

Here p counts every term, including the interaction. An interaction uses up a degree of freedom like any other predictor.

2.3Interpreting slopes with an interaction

Collect every term that contains \(X_1\):

\[Y = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 + \beta_3X_2)\,X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \epsilon\]

The effect of a one-unit increase in \(X_1\) is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3X_2\): a formula, not a single number. Rearranging the other way, the effect of \(X_2\) is \(\beta_2 + \beta_3X_1\).

β₁ on its ownX₁'s slope only when X₂ = 0. That's often unrealistic or outside the data.
β₃ > 0X₁'s effect gets stronger as X₂ increases.
β₃ < 0X₁'s effect gets weaker as X₂ increases. It doesn't mean Y goes down.

Factory

Units produced from the number of production lines and workers:

\[\hat Y = 1.2 + 3.4X_{lines} + 0.22X_{workers} + 1.4X_{lines}X_{workers} = 1.2 + (3.4 + 1.4X_{workers})X_{lines} + 0.22X_{workers}\]

Each extra line adds 3.4 + 1.4 × workers units: 17.4 units with 10 workers. The interaction was justified before any fitting, because a line needs workers to produce anything. A reason from the subject makes an interaction more believable than one found by testing every possible term.

2.4The hierarchy principle

An interaction can be highly significant while the main effects it's built from are not. In Assignment 1: TEMP (p = 0.59) and PROD (p = 0.27) next to TEMP × PROD (p < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶), and AIRFLOW (p = 0.70) next to two significant AIRFLOW interactions.

The rule: if an interaction is in the model, keep both of its main effects, whatever their p-values.

Why

  1. The main effect's p-value tests the wrong thing. Once X₁X₂ is in the model, β₁ is X₁'s slope when X₂ = 0. Whether that one slope is zero rarely matters, especially if X₂ = 0 is outside the data.
  2. Dropping a main effect distorts the interaction. X₁X₂ is usually correlated with X₁ and X₂. Remove X₁ and the interaction term has to absorb its job, which changes its meaning.
TermHow you decide
An interaction X₁X₂Its own t-test
A main effect that's in a kept interactionKeep it, whatever its p-value
A main effect that's not in any kept interactionIts own t-test, as in an additive model (like RPM in Assignment 1 Q3.4)
Check yourself
  1. A model has X₁, X₂, X₃, X₁X₂ and X₂X₃, and both interactions are significant. Which main effects stay?All three. X₁ is in X₁X₂, X₂ is in both interactions, and X₃ is in X₂X₃.
  2. The only interaction is X₁X₂ (significant). X₁ has p = 0.35 and X₃ has p = 0.40. What do you drop?Drop X₃: it's not in any interaction, so its own t-test decides. Keep X₁: it's part of X₁X₂.

Model selection procedures

Choosing predictors when there are many candidates: stepwise, backward and forward selection, best subsets with Cₚ, AIC and BIC, then interactions and curves. Executive salary, credit balance and clerical hours.

4.1Why model selection

Each candidate predictor is either in or out of the model, so p predictors give 2ᵖ possible additive models: 32 for 5 predictors, 1,024 for 10, over a trillion for 40. That's before interactions or squared terms. You need a systematic way to choose.

Executive salary data (n = 100)

QuantitativeDummy (1 = yes)
Years of experience, years of education, employees supervised, corporate assets ($M), age, company profits ($M), company sales ($M)Bonus eligible, board member, international responsibility

The general process

  1. Specify the maximum model: every candidate predictor.
  2. Choose a strategy: t-tests (stepwise, backward, forward) or a criterion (adjusted R², Cₚ, AIC, BIC).
  3. Evaluate the chosen model: then test interactions and curves.
Remember: p predictors → 2ᵖ models (2, not p, because each predictor has two states: in or out).

4.2Forward, backward and stepwise

MethodStarts withEach stepWeakness
ForwardNo predictorsAdd the remaining predictor with the smallest p-value, if it's below the cutoffOnce in, a predictor never leaves, even if it becomes useless later
BackwardAll predictorsRemove the predictor with the largest p-value, if it's above the cutoffOnce out, a predictor never returns; needs n well above the number of predictors
StepwiseNo predictorsAdd the best predictor, then re-check everything already in and remove any that became weakFollows one path, so it can still miss the best model

The re-check matters because predictors overlap. Once a second predictor is in, the first one's t-test only measures what it adds beyond the second, which may be little (newspaper once radio was in).

The cutoffs

library(olsrr)
step_mod = ols_step_both_p(full_model, p_enter = 0.1, p_remove = 0.3, details = TRUE)   # stepwise
back_mod = ols_step_backward_p(full_model, p_val = 0.2, details = TRUE)                # backward
fwd_mod  = ols_step_forward_p(full_model, p_val = 0.2, details = FALSE)                # forward
summary(step_mod$model)                                                              # the selected model
p_enterStepwise: the best remaining predictor enters only if its p-value is below this.
p_removeStepwise: a predictor already in is dropped if its p-value rises above this. Set higher than p_enter, so predictors don't bounce in and out.
p_valForward: the entry bar. Backward: the exit bar. One cutoff, because each goes one way only.

A higher cutoff is more lenient (0.2 keeps more predictors than 0.05). It only matters for predictors whose p-values fall between the two values. The full model you pass in is just the list of candidates.

Which method to use

  • If the question names one, use it.
  • Otherwise default to stepwise; backward works well when n is large relative to the number of predictors; forward when there are too many predictors to fit at once; best subsets when there are only a few.
  • Run more than one. If they agree, you can be confident; if not, look closely at the predictors they differ on.
Remember: forward and stepwise start empty and add; only backward starts full. Stepwise is forward with a re-check.

4.3Cautions

  • Many tests, many false positives. Automatic methods run a large number of t-tests, so some predictors can get in by chance. Use them to screen a long list, not as proof of what truly matters.
  • They can break the hierarchy principle when interactions are candidates: check every kept interaction's main effects are in the model.
  • They can split a category's dummies (e.g. keep the Asian dummy without the Caucasian one). Keep or drop a category's dummies together.

R also has MASS::stepAIC(), which runs forward, backward or stepwise selection using AIC instead of p-values.

4.4Criteria: adjusted R², Cₚ, AIC, BIC

All-possible-regressions fits every model (or the best model of each size) and scores each one with a criterion.

CriterionBetter isPenalty for extra predictors
R²HigherNone: always rises, so it can't choose between sizes
Adjusted R² / SₑHigher / lower (equivalent)Light: tends to allow a slightly bigger model
Mallows' CₚLower, and close to the number of parameters (p + 1)Moderate
AICLowerModerate
BICLowerHeaviest: tends to pick the smallest model
\[C_p = \frac{SSE_p}{MSE_{full}} + 2(p+1) - n\] \[AIC = n\ln\!\left(\frac{SSE}{n}\right) + 2(p+1)\] \[BIC = n\ln\!\left(\frac{SSE}{n}\right) + (p+1)\ln(n)\]

Here p is the number of predictors, so p + 1 counts the parameters including the intercept. For the full model, Cₚ equals p + 1 exactly. A subset with Cₚ well above p + 1 is probably missing something important (biased).

When criteria disagree

  1. Look for a size that most criteria accept.
  2. Fit the candidate models and check whether the extra terms are actually significant.
  3. Parsimony: when two models fit about equally well, prefer the simpler one: less overfitting, easier to interpret, more reliable estimates. "About equally" means a gain that's small in practical terms (Sₑ falling by under a dollar out of 63, not 63 to 50).
Remember: adjusted R² is the only criterion here where higher is better. Sₑ, Cₚ, AIC and BIC: lower is better.

How regsubsets works

Regression diagnostics

Checking the model's assumptions so you know whether to trust its results: linearity, independence, equal variance, normality, multicollinearity and outliers. Advertising, clerical hours, credit balance and executive salary.

5.1Residuals and the six checks

A residual is what the model failed to explain: observed minus predicted, \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). If a store sold 20 units and the model predicted 18, the residual is 2. SSE is just all the residuals squared and added up; diagnostics look at them one by one, on plots.

If the model is good, the residuals should look like random noise. A pattern means the model has missed something real, and the tests and intervals from earlier lectures can't be fully trusted.

5.3Independence

Each error should stand on its own. Example: a model predicts daily electricity use, and a cold snap it doesn't know about makes it under-predict on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday. Knowing Monday's error tells you about Tuesday's, so the errors aren't independent.

Why it matters: R treats every row as fresh information. With linked errors there's less real information than it looks, so standard errors are too small and p-values look more significant than they are.

  • Causes: serial correlation (time series), spatial association, clusters.
  • Check: plot residuals against time/observation order, location or group. Runs of positives then negatives signal a problem; a random zig-zag is fine.
  • For data not collected over time, space or groups (like Advertising), independence is usually assumed. Keep it in mind when choosing a project dataset.

5.4Equal variance (homoscedasticity)

The residuals should spread about the same amount everywhere, an even rectangle. A funnel (tight at one end, fanning out at the other) is heteroscedasticity. "Homo" = same, "hetero" = different.

The Breusch-Pagan test

\[H_0: \text{homoscedasticity (equal variance)} \qquad H_a: \text{heteroscedasticity}\]

The null is the good case, so you want a large p-value. By hand: regress the squared residuals on the model's terms, then

\[\chi^2_{BP} = n R^2_{aux} \sim \chi^2_{k}\]

where k is the number of terms in that auxiliary regression. Squaring the residuals measures the spread at each point; if the predictors can explain it, the spread changes with them.

aux = lm(residuals(model)^2 ~ X2 + I(X2^2) + X4 + X5, data = clerical.df)
chi = nrow(clerical.df) * summary(aux)$r.squared
1 - pchisq(chi, df = 4)    # matches bptest()

Results

ModelBP p-valueDecision
Advertising cubic0.00036 (χ² = 200 × 0.1147 = 22.94, df 5)Reject: unequal variance, even though the plots looked fine
Advertising, TV up to the 11th power0.307Fail to reject (a demonstration, not a recommended fix)

Plots and tests can disagree. The test can catch subtle problems the eye misses, so look at the plot, then confirm with the test.

Fixing unequal variance

  1. Transform Y: log(Y), or √Y for counts. This squashes large values and evens out the spread.
  2. Box-Cox finds a suitable power transformation (later in the course).
  3. Weighted least squares, when you know how the variance changes.
Remember: powers of X fix curves; transforming Y fixes funnels. For BP, a large p-value is good news.

5.5Normality

The residuals should follow a normal (bell-shaped) distribution. On a Q-Q plot, the points should hug the diagonal line.

  • Bow shape away from the line: skewness.
  • S shape: kurtosis (heavy or light tails).
  • A few points far off at the ends: possible outliers.
ggplot(df, aes(sample = residuals(model))) + stat_qq() + stat_qq_line(col = "red")
hist(residuals(model))
shapiro.test(residuals(model))
\[H_0: \text{the residuals are normally distributed} \qquad H_a: \text{they are not}\]

Again the null is the good case. Advertising (11th-power model): p = 0.313. Clerical quadratic model: p = 0.856, and the Q-Q points hug the line, with a slight wobble at the ends that's normal for a small sample.

Remember: Shapiro-Wilk tests normality; Breusch-Pagan tests equal variance. Both: large p-value = assumption holds.

5.6Multicollinearity

Two or more predictors are strongly related to each other, so they carry overlapping information (a truck's load and horsepower). Being correlated with Y is good; being correlated with other predictors is the problem.

Effects: coefficients swing wildly depending on what else is in the model, standard errors inflate, and p-values become unreliable.

\[VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_j}\]

where \(R^2_j\) comes from regressing predictor j on all the other predictors.

VIFMeaning
1No collinearity
1 to 5Moderate; no action needed
Above 5 (or 10)Serious: coefficients poorly estimated, p-values questionable
library(car);    vif(model)
library(mctest); imcdiag(model, method = "VIF")   # also flags collinear pairs

Use main effects only. X and X², or X and an interaction with X, are related by construction; that collinearity can be ignored (centering the variables leaves their p-values unchanged).

Examples

  • Advertising: TV and radio, r = 0.055, VIF = 1.003.
  • Clerical: VIFs 1.003 (X2), 1.247 (X4), 1.246 (X5). The X4–X5 correlation of 0.443 shows up: \(1/(1 - 0.443^2) \approx 1.24\).

Practice 4–5: credit balance

In Balance ~ Income + Rating + Age + Limit + Cards + Student, Rating (VIF 230.9) and Limit (229.2) are collinear: banks set limits largely from ratings, so their scatterplot is almost a straight line. Every other VIF was below 3.

Dropping Limit brought Rating's VIF down to 2.72, with every VIF under 3. Cards then had p = 0.267, so it was dropped too (adjusted R² stayed at 0.9499):

\[\widehat{Balance} = -547.30 - 7.798\,Income + 3.981\,Rating - 0.624\,Age + 417.51\,Student \qquad S_e = 102.9\]

Fixes: drop one of the collinear predictors (its information is redundant), or combine them into one variable (e.g. the average of standardized Limit and Rating as "creditworthiness").

Remember: VIF above 5–10 between predictors is a problem; ignore VIF between a term and its own powers or interactions.

5.7Outliers, leverage and influence

IdeaMeaningMeasure
OutlierA point far from the rest, often with a big residual (unusual Y)Residual plots
LeverageUnusual X values, far out to the side\(h_i\) (hatvalues)
InfluenceRemoving the point changes the fitted model a lotCook's distance \(D_i\)

The seesaw: the regression line balances on the middle of the X values. A point near the middle can't tilt it much. A point far out at the edge (high leverage) can, but only does if it's also off the line. Influence needs both unusual X and a big residual. A high-leverage point that sits on the trend just confirms the line.

Cook's distance

df[cooks.distance(model) > 0.5, ]      # rows above 0.5
plot(model, which = 4)                 # bar per row; R labels the 3 largest
plot(model, which = 5)                 # residuals vs leverage, with Cook's contours
  • Above 1: influential. Above 0.5: worth investigating.
  • 4/n (n = number of rows) or 3 × the mean D: stricter screening lines. A bar just over them means "take a look", not "delete".
  • The cutoff depends only on n, not on the outliers: it's a fixed line across the plot.

Leverage

lev = hatvalues(model)
p = length(coef(model))        # number of coefficients, intercept included
n = nrow(df)
lev[lev > 2*p/n];  lev[lev > 3*p/n]

Leverage is a warning flag: it tells you which points could pull the line; Cook's tells you which ones did. A high-leverage point pulls the line toward itself, so its own residual can look deceptively small.

Clerical (Practice 6), Y ~ X2 + X2² + X4 + X5

  • Cook's: the largest bar is row 5 at about 0.07, below 4/52 ≈ 0.077 and far below 0.5. (In the model without X2², row 52 was largest at about 0.08.) No influential points.
  • Leverage (p = 5, n = 52): above 2p/n ≈ 0.19: rows 5, 9, 23, 29, 41, 52; above 3p/n ≈ 0.29: rows 5 (0.38), 9 (0.35), 41 (0.35). Unusual X values, but they sit near the line, so they aren't influential.

What to do with an influential point

Check whether it's a recording or measurement error (e.g. 28 typed as 82); if so, correct or remove it. Otherwise refit without it and see how much the coefficients change, and report both. Don't delete a point just because it's unusual.

Remember: leverage = unusual X; influence = unusual X and off the line. Cook's above 0.5–1 is the real warning.

5.8The order of fixes

Curve first (linearity)Add X², then X³ if it still curves. Stop when the residual plot is flat or the new term isn't significant.
Then spread (equal variance)Scale-location plot and BP test. If BP fails, transform Y (log, √, Box-Cox) and re-check.
Then normalityQ-Q plot and Shapiro-Wilk.
MulticollinearityVIF on the main effects; drop or combine collinear predictors.
OutliersCook's distance and leverage on the full final model.

Use the simplest model that passes the checks, whatever power that turns out to be. Run linearity, BP, Shapiro and Cook's on the full model you'll actually use; VIF is the one check run on main effects only.

R4Mistakes to avoid

Saying X "causes" YObservational data shows association. Say "is associated with".
Interpreting without units or "holding constant"$1,000 on TV → about 46 units, holding radio and newspaper constant.
Getting R² backwardsIt's the share of the variation in Y explained by the model, not "the model explained by X".
Calling R² the correlationr is the correlation; R² is the coefficient of determination.
Giving X p + 1 rowsRows = n observations; columns = p + 1 parameters.
Dividing SSE by the wrong dfWrite n − p − 1 first, then divide. MSE slips are the most common arithmetic error.
Adjusted R² higher than R²Impossible. Recheck the arithmetic.
Numbers that contradict each otherA high R² with F ≈ 1 means something was miscalculated.
Running t-tests after a non-significant global FStop. The model isn't useful.
Thinking aov() SS don't depend on orderThey're sequential. t-tests in summary() aren't.
"The dropped predictors matter because SSE went up"SSE always goes up. Ask whether F beats the critical value.
Building the reduced model from the tested predictorsReduced = full model minus the predictors being tested.
Choosing what to drop by |t| < 1Drop the largest p-value first. |t| < 1 tells you whether adjusted R² will rise.
Comparing models with plain R²Use adjusted R² and RSE.
Confidence interval for one new caseUse a prediction interval.
Saying "if it's in range" without checkingApply the rule to the numbers given and commit to an answer.
Interpreting β₁ alone in an interaction modelIt's only X₁'s slope when X₂ = 0. Use β₁ + β₃X₂ with a realistic X₂.
Reading a negative interaction as "Y decreases"It means X₁'s effect gets weaker as X₂ grows, not that Y goes down.
Dropping a main effect that's in a kept interactionHierarchy principle: keep it, whatever its p-value.
Keeping a main effect just because others are protectedA main effect in no kept interaction is judged by its own t-test.
Dropping a predictor before testing its interactionsUnless told where to start, test interactions among all predictors (X₁ in the sales problem).
Dropping several interactions at onceOne at a time, largest p first. p-values shift after each refit.
Trusting adjusted R² over the t-testAdjusted R² rises whenever |t| > 1, which is a lower bar than significance.
Using ≠ for a "does it increase?" questionThat's one-sided: Hₐ: β > 0, with a one-tailed p-value.
Reusing a template without changing every numberRead the decision back against your own p-value.
Forgetting factor() on a coded categoryRank 1/2/3 would get one slope, as if it were a measurement.
Calling a dummy coefficient "an increase"It's a difference between a group and the baseline.
Dropping one dummy of a categoryKeep or drop all of a category's dummies together; test them with a partial F-test.
Comparing two non-baseline groups from the outputR only prints comparisons with the baseline. Subtract the two coefficients.
Mismatched labels in ifelse()Check with unique() first: the credit data has " Male" with a leading space.
Getting the units of a predictor wrongCards is one card, income is $1,000, age is one year. Ask what one unit is.
Forgetting to scale the changeThe coefficient is for one unit. For 10 units or 3 cards, multiply.
"A balance of −7.80"A negative coefficient is a decrease: "about $7.80 less".
Replacing instead of adding in an interactionThe group's slope is the main effect plus the interaction.
Mixing up the quadratic shapesPositive β₂: U with a lowest point. Negative: ∩ with a highest point.
Interpreting β₁ in a quadratic as a slopeWith X² in the model, β₁ alone has no useful meaning.
Adding powers until adjusted R² stops risingStop when the new top term isn't significant.
Counting models as pᵖEach predictor is in or out: 2ᵖ models.
Thinking stepwise starts with the full modelStepwise and forward start empty; only backward starts full.
Getting the cutoff direction backwardsA higher p_val (0.2) is more lenient and keeps more predictors than 0.05.
Choosing a model size by R²R² always favours the biggest model. Use adjusted R², Cₚ, AIC or BIC.
Treating the selected model as proofAutomatic methods run many tests; use them to screen, then check the result.
Accepting a split category or broken hierarchyCheck selected models keep all of a category's dummies and every interaction's main effects.
Picking the bigger model for a tiny gainParsimony: when fits are about equal, prefer the simpler model.
Forgetting nvmaxregsubsets stops at 8 predictors by default.
Thinking a negative t gives a different p-valueOnly |t| matters: t = −2.02 and +2.02 give the same p-value.
Plotting residuals against one X in a multiple regressionPlot against the fitted values; use each X only to find which predictor curves.
Wanting a small p-value from BP or ShapiroTheir nulls are the good case (equal variance, normality): a large p-value means the assumption holds.
Squaring X to fix a funnelPowers of X fix curves; transform Y (log, √, Box-Cox) to fix unequal spread.
Trusting the plot over the testThe Advertising cubic looked fine but failed BP. Use both.
Running VIF with X² or interactions includedThey're collinear with their main effects by construction. Use main effects only.
Calling every high-leverage point a problemInfluence needs unusual X and a big residual. Check Cook's distance.
Deleting a point because it's above 4/nThat's a "take a look" line. Remove only data errors; otherwise compare fits with and without it.

R6How to phrase interpretations

A number predictor"Holding [the others] constant, each additional [one unit, with its real unit] of X is associated with an [increase/decrease] of about [coefficient, with units] in average Y."
Example: "…each additional credit card is associated with an increase of about $18.21 in average balance."
A dummy"Holding [the others] constant, [group coded 1] have an average Y about [coefficient] [higher/lower] than [baseline]."
Example: "Students carry an average balance about $425.61 higher than non-students."
An interaction"The effect of X depends on Z: each extra unit of X is associated with a change in Y of about [β_X + β_XZ × Z]." Then plug in a real Z.
Example: "Each extra $1,000 on TV adds about 19.1 + 1.1 × radio units; at $30k radio, about 52."
The intercept"The predicted Y when all predictors are 0 [and every category is at its baseline]. It [has / has no] practical meaning because…"
Example: "−493.73 has no practical meaning: nobody in the data has age 0, and a negative balance is impossible."
A squared term"The positive/negative X² term means the curve opens upward/downward, with a lowest/highest point."
Example: "β̂₂ < 0: quality rises, peaks, then falls as pressure increases."

Check every time

  1. Units: what is one unit? One card, $1,000 of income, one year of age.
  2. Size of the change: the coefficient is per one unit. For any other change, multiply.
  3. Direction: a minus sign is a decrease. Say "about $7.80 less", not "a balance of −7.80".
  4. Dummies are differences from the baseline, not increases.
  5. Say what's held constant, and use "associated with", not "causes".
  6. Interactions: add, don't replace. The slope is the main effect plus the interaction term.
  7. Several changes at once (additive model): work out each piece, then add them.
