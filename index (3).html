Lecture 1

Multiple linear regression

The toddler height example, the Advertising dataset (200 markets) and first-order models with quantitative predictors.

1.1Simple regression: three versions of "the model"

We have pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) and want a straight line describing how \(Y\) changes with \(X\). \(X\) is the predictor (independent, explanatory variable). \(Y\) is the response (dependent variable).

ExpressionWhat it is
\(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\)Population model. Describes each individual observation, scatter included. The β's are true but unknown.
\(E(Y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\)Regression equation. The average Y at a given X. ε disappears because \(E(\epsilon) = 0\).
\(\hat{Y} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X\)Estimated model. The \(\hat\beta\)'s are calculated from our sample by least squares.

\(\beta_0\) is the intercept (Y when X = 0), \(\beta_1\) is the slope (change in Y per 1-unit increase in X), and \(\epsilon\) is everything affecting Y that isn't X.

Toddler example (12 toddlers, age 18–29 months)

\[\widehat{Height} = 60.25 + 0.848 \times Age\]

For each extra month of age, predicted (average) height increases by about 0.85 cm. The intercept, 60.25 cm at age 0, has no practical meaning: no toddler in the data is close to 0 months.

Remember: β is the true unknown value; β̂ is our estimate from the sample. We need the population model to say what we're trying to learn, and the estimated model is what we can actually compute.
Check yourself
  1. Interpret the 0.85 in plain English.For each extra month of age, predicted (average) height goes up by about 0.85 cm. Say "on average": the line describes the average toddler, not any one child.
  2. Why shouldn't this model predict a 6-month-old's height?The data only cover 18–29 months, so 6 months is extrapolation. Babies grow much faster in their first year, so the same line probably doesn't hold there.

1.2Sums of squares, R² and MSE

Take any data point and picture three vertical distances:

NameFormulaMeasures
SST (total)\(\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2\)How far the points are from the mean: total variation in Y
SSR (regression)\(\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2\)How far the line is from the mean: variation the model explains
SSE (error)\(\sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\)How far the points are from the line: variation left unexplained
\[SST = SSR + SSE\]

BI analogy: if your only "model" were AVG(Height) (80.175 cm here), SST measures how wrong that flat prediction is. The regression line removes part of the error (SSR), and what's left is SSE.

R², the coefficient of determination

\[R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}\]

R² is the proportion of the variation in Y explained by the model. For the toddlers: SST = 118.2, SSE = 15.4, SSR = 102.8, so R² = 0.87. "87% of the variation in height is explained by age."

Say this"75% of the variation in height is explained by the model."
Not this"75% of the model is explained by the variation in age."

MSE, the estimate of the error variance

\[MSE = \frac{SSE}{n-2}\]

We divide by \(n-2\) because two parameters (\(\beta_0, \beta_1\)) were estimated, and each uses up one degree of freedom. That makes MSE an unbiased estimate of \(\text{Var}(\epsilon)\). With p predictors the rule becomes \(n - p - 1\).

Remember: SST = SSR + SSE, and R² = SSR / SST is the share of the variation in Y that the model explains.
Check yourself
  1. SST = 200 and SSE = 50. Find SSR and R², and interpret R².SSR = 150 and R² = 0.75. 75% of the variation in Y is explained by the model; the other 25% is unexplained.
  2. A model has 3 predictors and n = 50. What do you divide SSE by to get MSE?\(n - p - 1 = 50 - 3 - 1 = 46\).
  3. Is R² the correlation coefficient?No. The correlation is r (between −1 and 1). R² is the coefficient of determination. In simple regression only, R² = r².

1.3Advertising: one medium at a time

200 markets. Response sale is sales in thousands of units. Predictors tv, radio and newspaper are advertising budgets in thousands of dollars.

Advertising = read.table("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DanikaLipman/DATA603/main/Advertising.txt",
                         header = TRUE, sep = "\t")
summary(lm(sale ~ tv, data = Advertising))
confint(lm(sale ~ tv, data = Advertising))
ModelEquationrF (1, 198)p95% CI for slope
TV7.03 + 0.0475·TV0.78312.1≈ 0(0.0422, 0.0528)
Radio9.31 + 0.2025·Radio0.5898.42≈ 0(0.1622, 0.2427)
Newspaper12.35 + 0.0547·News0.2310.890.001(0.0220, 0.0874)

Each model tests \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (no linear relationship) against \(H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0\). Worked for newspaper:

\[F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} = \frac{282.34/1}{5134.805/198} = 10.89\]

The df are (1, 198): 1 predictor on top, \(n - 2 = 198\) underneath. On their own, all three media look significant.

Interpreting with units

TV slope 0.0475: every extra $1,000 on TV is associated with about 47.5 more units sold, on average. This is observational data, so say "associated with", not "causes".

The problem

Markets that spend more on radio also tend to spend more on newspaper; the predictors are correlated. Radio really drives sales, so the newspaper-only model gives newspaper credit for radio's effect. Newspaper acts as a stand-in for radio.

Remember: a one-predictor model can't separate overlapping effects. Multiple regression can, because it estimates each effect while holding the others constant.
Check yourself
  1. Why does each simple F-test have df (1, 198)?1 = the number of predictors. 198 = 200 observations minus the 2 estimated parameters (β₀ and β₁).
  2. Radio's 95% CI is (0.1622, 0.2427). How can you tell it's significant at α = 0.05 without the p-value?0 is not in the interval, so we reject H₀: β₁ = 0 at α = 0.05. A 95% CI and a two-sided test at 0.05 always agree.
  3. Newspaper is significant alone but not alongside TV and radio. Why?Newspaper is correlated with radio. Alone, it borrows radio's effect; once radio is held constant, it has nothing left to explain. This idea returns later as multicollinearity.

1.4The multiple regression model

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p + \epsilon = \mu + \epsilon\]

\(p\) is the number of predictors and \(\mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \dots + \beta_pX_p\) is the deterministic part, the predictable average. For Advertising:

\[Sales = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{TV} + \beta_2 X_{radio} + \beta_3 X_{newspaper} + \epsilon\]

A first-order model has each quantitative predictor on its own, to the power 1: no \(X^2\) terms and no \(X_1X_2\) products.

Why it's harder than simple regression

  1. Model selection: many possible subsets of predictors, several of which can look reasonable.
  2. Visualization: you can't plot beyond 3D.
  3. Overfitting: more predictors isn't always better; complex models fit noise.
  4. Computation: needs matrix algebra, which R does for you.

Geometry: 1 predictor gives a line, 2 give a plane through a 3D cloud, 3+ give a hyperplane. Least squares always minimizes the squared vertical distances to the surface.

Remember: "first-order" is about powers and products, not the number of predictors.
Check yourself
  1. Is \(Sales = \beta_0 + \beta_1 TV + \beta_2 TV^2\) first-order?No, because of the TV² term, which makes it second-order. Simple regression has one predictor and is first-order.

1.5Least squares and the design matrix

Choose the \(\hat\beta\)'s that minimize SSE:

\[SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \hat\beta_p x_{pi})\big)^2\]

The solution in matrix form:

\[\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\] \[X = \begin{pmatrix}1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np}\end{pmatrix}\]
ObjectAdvertising sizeWhy
\(y\)200 × 1One response per market
\(X\)200 × 4One row per market; columns 1, TV, radio, newspaper
\(X'X\)4 × 4The (p+1) × (p+1) matrix that gets inverted
\(\hat\beta\)4 × 1One estimate per parameter

The column of 1s produces the intercept, because \(\beta_0\) is multiplied by 1 on every row. Without it, the plane is forced through the origin, which distorts the slopes too.

X = model.matrix(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising)
y = Advertising$sale
solve(t(X) %*% X) %*% t(X) %*% y   # same as coef(lm(...))

What happens to the slopes

PredictorSlope aloneSlope in multiple model
TV0.04750.0458
Radio0.20250.1885
Newspaper0.0547−0.0010

TV barely moves because it is nearly uncorrelated with the other media. Newspaper collapses because it is correlated with radio.

Remember: X has n rows (observations) and p + 1 columns (parameters).
Check yourself
  1. What are the dimensions of X for Advertising?200 × 4. 200 markets; columns for the intercept, TV, radio, newspaper. (X′X is 4 × 4.)
  2. What goes wrong without the column of 1s?There's no intercept, so the fitted plane is forced through the origin (predicted Y = 0 when all X = 0), which also distorts the slopes.

1.7Confidence intervals for coefficients

\[\hat\beta_i \pm t_{\alpha/2,\; n-p-1} \times S_{\hat\beta_i}\]

\(S_{\hat\beta_i}\) is the standard error (the Std. Error column of summary()). We use a t-distribution because the error variance is estimated, not known. \(t_{\alpha/2}\) is the upper critical value (positive).

By hand: TV at 95%

df = 200 − 3 − 1 = 196, so \(t_{0.025,196} \approx 1.972\). Then \(0.045765 \pm 1.972 \times 0.001395 = 0.045765 \pm 0.00275\), giving (0.0430, 0.0485).

"We are 95% confident that each extra $1,000 on TV is associated with an increase of 43 to 48.5 units sold, holding radio and newspaper constant."

confint(predict_ad_model)                # 95%
confint(predict_ad_model, level = 0.99)  # 99%
Levelt (df 196)TV interval
95%1.97(0.0430, 0.0485)
99%2.60(0.0421, 0.0494)

"95% confident" means: if we repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true β. It doesn't mean a 95% chance that β is in this particular interval.

Newspaper's CI is (−0.0126, 0.0105). It contains 0, so its effect isn't distinguishable from zero. Before dropping it, confirm with a t-test or partial F-test, compare adjusted R² and RSE, and ask whether there's a business reason to keep it.

Remember: 0 inside the CI ⇔ fail to reject H₀: βᵢ = 0. Higher confidence means a bigger t, so a wider interval.
Check yourself
  1. Is the 99% CI wider or narrower than the 95% CI? Why?Wider. The t critical value grows from about 1.97 to 2.60, and multiplies the same standard error. More confidence costs precision.

1.8p-values, α and errors

TermMeaning
p-valueProbability of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true
αP(Type I error): rejecting H₀ when it's actually true (a false alarm). Usually 0.05.
Type II errorFailing to reject H₀ when it's actually false (a missed effect)
Decision ruleReject H₀ when p-value < α

The notes use β for the Type II error probability. That's a different β from the regression coefficients.

Say this"p < 0.05, so we reject H₀. There is evidence that…"
Not this"We have enough evidence for H₀" or "we proved H₀ is false."
Remember: we reject or fail to reject H₀; we never prove it or gather evidence for it.

1.9The global F-test and the ANOVA table

The first question: is the model useful at all?

\[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_p = 0 \qquad H_a: \text{at least one } \beta_i \neq 0\]

H₀ says the model is no better than the null model, lm(sale ~ 1), which predicts ȳ every time. For the null model SSR = 0 and SSE = SST.

Why test the whole model first?

Many separate t-tests inflate false positives: about 5 "significant" results per 100 useless predictors at α = 0.05. The global F-test is one test, so the overall Type I error rate stays at α.

SourceSSdfMean squareF
RegressionSSR\(p\)MSR = SSR / pMSR / MSE
ErrorSSE\(n-p-1\)MSE = SSE / (n−p−1)
TotalSST\(n-1\)
\[F_{obs} = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-p-1)} \sim F_{p,\;n-p-1}\]

If the predictors are useless, MSR ≈ MSE and F ≈ 1. A large F means the model explains far more than chance.

Advertising, from aov()

            Sum Sq   Df
tv        3314.618    1
radio     1545.617    1
newspaper    0.089    1
Residuals  556.824  196
  • SSR = 3314.618 + 1545.617 + 0.089 = 4860.324; MSR = 4860.324 / 3 = 1620.11
  • MSE = 556.824 / 196 = 2.841
  • F = 570.3, df (3, 196), p ≈ 0, so we reject H₀
  • SST = 5417.149 and R² = 4860.324 / 5417.149 = 0.897

If the global test fails, stop. Don't hunt through t-tests for something significant. If it succeeds, at least one predictor matters, but it doesn't say which.

Remember: both the SS column and the df column add up: SST = SSR + SSE and (n−1) = p + (n−p−1). Write the df first, then divide.
Check yourself
  1. n = 30, p = 4, SSR = 600, SSE = 250. Fill in the ANOVA table.df: 4, 25, 29. MSR = 150, MSE = 250/25 = 10, F = 15, SST = 850, R² = 0.71.
  2. Sanity check: could R² = 0.71 go with F ≈ 1.2?No. R² = 0.71 says the model explains a lot; F ≈ 1 says it barely beats the mean. When numbers contradict each other, recheck the arithmetic.
  3. The global F-test gives p = 0.40. What next?Fail to reject H₀ and stop. Searching the t-tests would invite false positives. Rethink the predictors or the model.

1.10Sequential SS in aov()

aov() assigns sums of squares in the order the predictors are listed. The first predictor gets credit for everything it explains alone; each later one gets only the extra it adds.

Newspaper last
sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper
Newspaper SS = 0.089
Newspaper first
sale ~ newspaper + tv + radio
Newspaper SS = 282.34 (its simple-regression SSR)

The individual SS change with order, but the total SSR (4860.324) doesn't.

Remember: aov() SS depend on order; t-tests in summary() don't.

1.11Individual t-tests

\[H_0: \beta_i = 0 \;\text{(given the other predictors)} \qquad H_a: \beta_i \neq 0\] \[T_{obs} = \frac{\hat\beta_i - 0}{S_{\hat\beta_i}} \sim t_{n-p-1}\]

The test statistic counts how many standard errors the estimate is from 0. The p-value is two-sided, \(2 \times P(T \geq |t_{obs}|)\); R prints it as Pr(>|t|).

PredictorEstimateStd. ErrortpDecision
TV0.0457650.00139532.81≈ 0Keep

Remember: each t-test asks whether a predictor adds anything on top of all the others, so it isn't affected by the order in the formula.
Check yourself
  1. Compute newspaper's t from its estimate and SE.−0.001037 / 0.005871 = −0.177, df = 196, p = 0.86. Fail to reject: newspaper adds nothing significant given TV and radio.
  2. With newspaper listed first, does its t-test p-value change?No, it stays 0.86. Only the sequential aov() SS change with order.

1.12The partial F-test

Does a subset of q predictors add anything, given the others are already in the model?

  • Full model: all predictors
  • Reduced model: the full model minus the q predictors being tested
\[F_{obs} = \frac{(SSE_{reduced} - SSE_{full})/(df_{reduced} - df_{full})}{SSE_{full}/df_{full}} \sim F_{q,\;df_{full}}\]

df here means error df. Dropping predictors always increases SSE; the test asks whether it increases more than chance would explain.

reduced = lm(sale ~ tv + radio, data = Advertising)
full    = lm(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising)
anova(reduced, full)   # reduced model first
\[F = \frac{(556.9140 - 556.8253)/(197-196)}{556.8253/196} = \frac{0.0887}{2.841} = 0.031,\quad df = (1, 196),\; p = 0.86\]

Fail to reject, so drop newspaper. The final model is:

\[\widehat{Sale} = 2.921 + 0.04575X_{TV} + 0.18799X_{radio}\]

Three connections

  1. The SSE increase (0.0887) is newspaper's sequential SS (0.089), because newspaper was entered last.
  2. For one predictor, F = t²: (−0.177)² ≈ 0.031, with the same p-value.
  3. The partial F-test earns its keep when q > 1, e.g. radio and newspaper together beyond TV.
# Do radio and newspaper together add anything beyond TV? (q = 2)
anova(lm(sale ~ tv, data = Advertising),
      lm(sale ~ tv + radio + newspaper, data = Advertising))
Remember: the predictors you're testing are the ones missing from the reduced model. Report the df as a pair: (q, df_full).
Check yourself
  1. n = 40. Full model: 5 predictors, SSE = 120. Reduced: 3 predictors, SSE = 180. Test it.df_full = 34, df_reduced = 36, q = 2. F = (60/2) / (120/34) = 30 / 3.53 = 8.5 with df (2, 34). The 5% cutoff is about 4.1, so reject H₀: the dropped predictors matter. (Not "because SSE went up"—it always does.)
  2. To test radio and newspaper together beyond TV, what are the models and q?Full: TV + radio + newspaper. Reduced: TV only. q = 2.

1.13R², adjusted R² and RSE

R²

\[R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} = \text{Cor}(Y, \hat Y)^2\]

R² can never decrease when a predictor is added, even a useless one. It has no penalty for complexity, so it always favours the bigger model.

Adjusted R²

\[R^2_{adj} = 1 - \frac{SSE/(n-p-1)}{SST/(n-1)} = 1 - \frac{MSE}{SST/(n-1)}\]

Penalizes each extra predictor through its degrees of freedom, so it can go down. It's always ≤ R².

RSE, the residual standard error

\[s = RSE = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-p-1}} = \sqrt{MSE}\]

The typical size of a prediction error, in the same units as Y. Lower is better.

summary(full)$r.squared;     summary(reduced)$r.squared
summary(full)$adj.r.squared; summary(reduced)$adj.r.squared
sigma(full); sigma(reduced)
ModelSSEdfMSER²R²adjRSE
TV + radio + newspaper556.8251962.8410.897210.89561.686
TV + radio556.9141972.8270.897190.89621.681

Plain R² favours the full model by 0.00002. Adjusted R² and RSE both favour TV + radio. "Using TV and radio, our sales predictions are typically off by about 1,700 units."

Two useful facts

  1. Adjusted R² and RSE always agree on the same dataset: both are built from MSE.
  2. Dropping a predictor raises adjusted R² exactly when its |t| < 1. Plain R² goes down (or stays the same) whenever you drop one.
Remember: compare models with adjusted R² and RSE, never plain R². Report plain R² when you say "X% of the variation is explained".
Check yourself
  1. n = 30, p = 4, SSE = 250, SST = 850. Find R²adj and RSE.MSE = 250/25 = 10. SST/(n−1) = 850/29 = 29.31. R²adj = 1 − 10/29.31 = 0.659. RSE = √10 = 3.16. Adjusted R² (0.659) is below R² (0.71), as it must be.
  2. A predictor has t = 0.8. Drop it: what happens to R² and adjusted R²?Adjusted R² goes up (|t| < 1). Plain R² goes down or stays the same.

Another example: TV = 100, radio = 30 gives fit 13.14 and PI (9.81, 16.46), about 9,808 to 16,465 units.

What sets the width

\[\hat y \pm t \times \sqrt{s^2 + SE(\hat y)^2}\]

With n = 200, \(SE(\hat y)\) is small, so RSE dominates: 15.83 ± 1.97 × 1.681 gives (12.52, 19.14), almost R's answer. If RSE doubled, the interval would roughly double in width.

Remember: one new observation → prediction interval; the average → confidence interval. Averages are more predictable than individuals.

  2. A manager is opening one new store. Which interval?A prediction interval: it's one individual observation, not an average.

1.15Extrapolation

Only predict inside the range of the data used to fit the model. In Advertising, TV runs from about 0.7 to 296 and radio from 0 to about 50.

  • TV = 400 is outside the data, so the answer is no: we have no evidence the straight line holds there, and ad spending often has diminishing returns.
  • Hidden extrapolation: each value can be in range alone while the combination never appears (very high TV and very high radio together). Check a scatterplot of the predictors against each other.
summary(Advertising)                       # min / max of each column
plot(Advertising$tv, Advertising$radio)    # which combinations exist?
Remember: apply the range rule to the actual numbers and commit to an answer.

Why negative signs for floors and bedrooms? Holding living area fixed, an extra bedroom means carving the same space into smaller rooms, which buyers may not value. Bedrooms and living area carry overlapping information, just like newspaper and radio.

Remember: a surprising sign often means overlapping predictors; read it through "holding the others constant".

P4Condos: dropping bedrooms

condo_no_bed = lm(listprice ~ livingarea + floors + baths, data = condos)

summary(condo_no_bed)

\(H_0: \beta_{bedrooms} = 0\) given living area, floors and baths are in the model; \(H_a: \beta_{bedrooms} \neq 0\).

You can work out the result before running it:

  • F = t² = (−0.610)² ≈ 0.37, df (1, 10), p ≈ 0.556 (the same as the t-test), so fail to reject and drop bedrooms.
  • SSE_full = 6.82² × 10 ≈ 465.1; SSE_reduced ≈ 465.1 + 0.372 × 46.51 ≈ 482.4 on 11 df.
  • New MSE ≈ 43.9, so RSE ≈ 6.62 (down from 6.82).
  • SST ≈ 465.1 / (1 − 0.9716) ≈ 16,377, so adjusted R² ≈ 0.9625 (up from 0.9603), as expected since |t| < 1.

To confirm: these are worked out from the full-model summary. Run the code and check the reduced model's output matches, then see whether living area, floors and baths all stay significant.

Remember: drop one predictor at a time and refit: p-values change when the model changes.

R4Mistakes to avoid

Saying X "causes" YObservational data shows association. Say "is associated with".
Interpreting without units or "holding constant"$1,000 on TV → about 46 units, holding radio and newspaper constant.
Getting R² backwardsIt's the share of the variation in Y explained by the model, not "the model explained by X".
Calling R² the correlationr is the correlation; R² is the coefficient of determination.
Giving X p + 1 rowsRows = n observations; columns = p + 1 parameters.
Dividing SSE by the wrong dfWrite n − p − 1 first, then divide. MSE slips are the most common arithmetic error.
Adjusted R² higher than R²Impossible. Recheck the arithmetic.
Numbers that contradict each otherA high R² with F ≈ 1 means something was miscalculated.
Running t-tests after a non-significant global FStop. The model isn't useful.
Thinking aov() SS don't depend on orderThey're sequential. t-tests in summary() aren't.
"The dropped predictors matter because SSE went up"SSE always goes up. Ask whether F beats the critical value.
Building the reduced model from the tested predictorsReduced = full model minus the predictors being tested.
Choosing what to drop by |t| < 1Drop the largest p-value first. |t| < 1 tells you whether adjusted R² will rise.
Comparing models with plain R²Use adjusted R² and RSE.
Confidence interval for one new caseUse a prediction interval.
Saying "if it's in range" without checkingApply the rule to the numbers given and commit to an answer.
